# Decision log

| # | Decision | Why | What I gave up |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Lead with two drivers' question, not with prices | The question "how much did you pay?" is the product problem. Prices are context. | A simpler "Uber vs Lyft" story |
| 2 | Dropped "Uber hides its cut" | Uber's driver pages show the fee on receipts and a weekly statement. The claim would be wrong and unfair. | A punchier headline |
| 3 | Framed take rate as a disagreement | Uber says about 21%; independent estimates run from a 29% median (London) to over 50% (three US drivers). Uber's number goes first. | A verdict |
| 4 | Matched trips only, quick pickups only | Raw averages mix trip lengths and discounted wait-longer rides | Coverage on thin routes (borough fallback instead) |
| 5 | Same month, year over year for waits | Rules out seasonality | Month-to-month resolution |
| 6 | Cut four early claims | They failed the matching or seasonality checks | Two of the most shareable charts |
| 7 | Dropped the airport angle | The data held up, but it wasn't the story drivers told me | A clean, narrow MVP |
| 8 | Built on Uber's existing weekly promise | A new promise is a pricing decision, not a product one. Making an existing one checkable is shippable. | A bolder pitch |
| 9 | Rejected "show the fare split on every receipt" | It already exists in some form; the gap is when and where drivers see it | |
| 10 | Added a held-out stress set | 98% on a golden set I wrote myself proved little | A flattering single number |
| 11 | No-AI keyword baseline on every eval | Shows what the model actually adds (95% vs 14% on stress routes) | |
| 12 | No city names in the personal story | Keeps the drivers unidentifiable. The data names its source so anyone can check it. | Some vividness |
