What would it cost Uber to promise drivers a ceiling on its cut?

A monthly cap: if Uber keeps more than the cap of what a driver's riders paid in a month, it pays the driver the difference. This is the "promise" arm of the pilot in my case study. Move the cap and the assumptions to see what it would have cost on 15.1 million real Uber trips.

New York City only, September 2026, public TLC trip records. A range, not a forecast.
35%

Public trip records have no driver IDs, so I grouped trips into driver-months of 160 trips three ways. Real drivers sit somewhere between these.

Why one trip can feel unfair while the month looks fine

Uber's share of each NYC trip, September 2026. The average is 22.1%, but the spread is wide: on 21.4% of trips Uber kept nothing or less, and on 18.6% it kept more than 40%. Drivers remember the second kind.

Share = (base fare − driver pay) ÷ base fare, both before tips, tolls, taxes and fees. Trips where Uber kept nothing are likely driven by NYC's driver pay floor and rider promotions, which public data can't separate.

What a driver in the promise arm would see

One screen on the earnings tab: this month's share so far, the promise line, and what happens if Uber goes over. The trip-level assistant from the other arm can link here: "this trip was 47%; your month so far is 24%."

Assumptions to challenge

  • NYC is a low-share city. Its driver pay floor keeps Uber's share near 22%. Where independent estimates run above 40%, the same cap would trigger far more often. A pilot would set the cap per market from local data.
  • A share isn't a wage. A fair share of a falling fare is still a pay cut, so the pilot measures dollars per hour, not just share.
  • 160 trips per month is a full-time pace. Part-time drivers have fewer trips, so their monthly share swings more and they'd trigger the cap more often.
  • No behavior change modeled. A promise could change which trips drivers accept. That's one reason to test it rather than ship it.
