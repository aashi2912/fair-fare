Summary

The information exists. It just isn't trusted where it matters.

The problem

Drivers in two countries weren't sure they were getting a fair share, and asked me what I paid. Uber already shows drivers its fee on receipts and in a weekly statement. The question still gets asked in the car, about one trip, when a rider names a number.

The evidence

On identical New York trips, Uber's price premium over Lyft shrank from 14% to below zero, while rides with 10+ minute pickups roughly doubled. What drivers keep is genuinely disputed: 21% by Uber's count, much more by independent estimates.

The bet

Uber already has an AI assistant for drivers. Teach it one new question, "the rider paid $X, I got $Y, why?", answered only from that trip's breakdown and honest when the split is bad. I built it, with evals.

The moment

It started in the back seat, not in a spreadsheet.

I open Lyft first. For my trips, it usually feels cheaper, so Uber is my second tab. One night I took an Uber anyway.

Near the end of the ride, my driver asked me a question. He wasn't sure he was getting a fair share of what riders pay.

Months later, in another country, a different driver had the same doubt and asked me the same question. Two drivers, two countries, one question. That's not an anecdote anymore. That's a pattern worth pulling on.

"Can I ask how much you paid?"Asked by two drivers, in two countries, months apart. Cities left out on purpose.

What they felt

Unsure they get a fair share of what riders pay.

What they did

Asked a rider, because the rider is the only other person who saw the price.

What it told me

Whatever Uber shows drivers today, it isn't settling the question in the car.

Oxford researchers estimate a 29% median in London. A Columbia analysis of three US drivers found over 50% in some cities. Uber attributes the gap to insurance and third-party fees.

The insight

Uber already discloses its cut. Disclosed isn't the same as trusted.

Uber's driver pages define its service fee as what the rider paid minus what the driver earned, excluding tips, tolls and certain fees and taxes. Since 2022 its weekly statements show how much customers paid and the week's service fee, and Uber's UK team says drivers can now see this in the app. So "drivers can't see the numbers" would be the wrong story, and an unfair one.

The gap is when and where the question comes up. A weekly statement answers it days later, in aggregate. My drivers were asking about one trip, in the moment, after hearing a number from a rider. Uber's own UK general manager has named that exact trap: comparing what a passenger says they paid with what the driver received. The rider's number often includes a tip, or is lower because of a discount the platform paid, so the gap looks bigger or smaller than it is.

And Uber already has the channel. Its AI driver assistant answers questions like "why did my earnings look different today?"

Uber built Uber Assistant with OpenAI to help drivers from onboarding to day-to-day earnings. The question my drivers asked is one it doesn't yet answer: why this trip's split looks the way it does.

The proposal

If I were Uber's PM: teach Uber Assistant one new question.

"The rider paid $X. I got $Y. Why?" Answered from that trip's own breakdown, at the moment the doubt shows up, and honest when the split is bad.

What ships

  • A new intent in the driver assistant, reachable from any trip's details or by voice.
  • The answer is built only from the trip ledger Uber already shows: what the rider paid, insurance, city and airport fees, taxes, tolls, rider discounts and the service fee.
  • The model writes the words, never the numbers. Amounts are filled in from the ledger, so the assistant can't misstate a dollar figure.
  • If the rider's number includes a tip or a platform-funded discount, it says so. If nothing on the trip explains it, it says it can't see that amount.

Why this, not a new screen

The data and the channel already exist, so this ships in a quarter. It builds on Uber's own transparency work instead of competing with it, and it targets the exact moment trust breaks: one trip, one number, right now.

The hard question, answered

"What if the honest answer is that the platform kept 47%?" Then it says 47%. The assistant can't fix a bad split, but it can end the guessing, and accurate bad news keeps more trust than rumor does. The trips that trigger the question also tell Uber exactly where pricing feels unfair.

The prototype

I built the assistant answer, and the evals that keep it honest.

The prototype answers drivers' questions about 40 synthetic trips: everyday rides, airport runs, rider discounts, quotes that include a tip, high-fee trips, trips with a missing line item, and quotes nothing can explain.

Every answer is checked automatically against the trip's numbers. I compared three designs: a no-AI template, a model that writes numbers itself, and the shipped design where the model writes only words and the app fills in every amount.

Decisions

What I chose, and what I threw out.

DecisionWhy
Dropped my first proposal, a weekly earnings meterUber already ships weekly statements with what riders paid and the service fee. Pitching it would have shown I hadn't checked.
Built on Uber Assistant instead of a new screenThe channel and the data exist. The gap is one unanswered question at one moment.
The model never writes numbersA single wrong dollar figure would destroy the trust the feature exists to build. Placeholders make that impossible by design.
Honest on bad splitsAn assistant that spins a 47% fee would make the problem worse. It states the share and never calls it "normal."
Dropped "Uber hides its cut"Uber shows the fee. The honest story is about trust, and it's the sharper one.
Presented take rate as a disagreement21% vs over 50% is contested. Uber's number goes first, the others are labeled estimates.
Matched trips, same month year over yearRemoves trip-length, discount and seasonal effects. Four early claims that failed these checks were cut.
Demoted the rider toolIt answers "which app is cheaper," which isn't the problem I'm solving, and on many routes the answer is Lyft.
No city names in the storyKeeps the drivers unidentifiable. The data names its source so anyone can check it.

Limits

What this doesn't prove.

The prototype runs on synthetic trips modeled on the fee categories Uber names publicly, not real ledgers, and 40 cases is a starting eval set, not a launch gate. Whether a clear answer actually moves trust, support contacts or hours is what the holdout test is for. The trip data is one city with a driver pay floor, and the wait-time rise has no known cause here. The Lyft study is preliminary. My two drivers are two people, which is why they're the reason I looked, not the evidence.
