Two drivers asked me what I paid. Here's how I'd find out what Uber should do about it.

A product case study: where the gap in rideshare pay really is, three ideas I tested against the evidence, and the pilot I'd run as Uber's PM to let data choose. With two working prototypes.

Summary

Uber's cut swings trip to trip, and US drivers have no ceiling on it.

The problem

Two drivers in two countries weren't sure they were getting a fair share, and asked what I paid. Since 2022, what riders pay and what drivers earn move separately, and Uber's share varies trip to trip by design.

The evidence

Nationally, Uber charges more and keeps more per trip than Lyft. In New York, Uber kept over 40% on 18.6% of its trips and nothing on 21.4%. Lyft caps its cut each month; Uber doesn't in the US.

The proposal

I tested three ideas against the evidence. Two survive, and either could be wrong. So instead of picking one, I'd run a four-group pilot: an explanation, a promise, both, or neither. I built a prototype for each.

The moment

It started in the back seat, not in a spreadsheet.

I open Lyft first. For my trips, it usually feels cheaper, so Uber is my second tab. One night I took an Uber anyway.

Near the end of the ride, my driver asked me a question. He wasn't sure he was getting a fair share of what riders pay.

Months later, in another country, a different driver had the same doubt and asked me the same question. Two drivers, two countries, one question. That's not an anecdote anymore. That's a pattern worth pulling on.

"Can I ask how much you paid?"Asked by two drivers, in two countries, months apart. Cities left out on purpose.

What they felt

Unsure they get a fair share of what riders pay.

What they did

Asked a rider, because the rider is the only other person who saw the price.

What it told me

Whatever Uber shows drivers today, it isn't settling the question in the car.

What this is, and isn't. The trip-level numbers are one city's public data, a city with a driver pay floor. My two drivers were elsewhere. National reports fill some of the gap, but with Uber's own data the answer could change. That's why my proposal is an experiment, not a verdict.

Three ideas

I tested three ideas against the evidence before picking one. Then I didn't pick one.

1. A weekly earnings meter

Show drivers what riders paid and what Uber kept each week.

Uber has shown exactly that in weekly statements since 2022, and its UK team says it's in the app.

Dropped: Uber already ships it
2. An AI that explains each trip

Teach Uber's driver assistant to answer "the rider paid $X, I got $Y, why?" I built it: 95% of 40 test trips pass every check.

But it explains the cut without changing it, and in some US markets Uber hides per-trip rider payments by choice.

Kept as one arm of the pilot
3. A ceiling on Uber's monthly cut

Promise drivers Uber keeps no more than a cap each month, like Lyft. On NYC data a 35% cap costs at most about 0.7% of fees.

But NYC has a pay floor, other cities would cost more, and a fair share of a falling fare is still a pay cut.

Kept as one arm of the pilot

Both survivors could work, and both could fail. An NYU study of Lyft's own pay promise found the guarantee and the transparency each had their own effect on drivers. So the honest proposal is a test that separates them.

The pilot

If I were Uber's PM: let a four-group pilot choose.

Randomly split drivers in two or three pilot markets into four groups for 12 weeks, including at least one market without a pay floor.

NeitherToday's experience. The comparison group.
Explanation onlyThe assistant answers "why did I get this?" for any trip, showing the trip and the month.
Promise onlyA monthly cap on Uber's share, set per market from local data, with top-ups after month end.

Across: explanation off / on. Down: promise off / on.

How I'd decide

  • Explanation works, promise adds little: ship the assistant. The problem was perception, and the fix is cheap.
  • Promise works, explanation adds little: ship the promise where its cost is below the value of the extra driver hours.
  • Both beat either alone: ship them together.
  • Nothing moves: it's the pay level, not fairness. Next, test pay for long pickups and traffic, which Lyft's own driver research named as top concerns.

What I'd watch for

Fees quietly raised elsewhere to fund the promise. Drivers changing which trips they accept. Spillover between groups in the same city, which is why market-level outcomes like wait times are read against holdout markets.

Why test instead of pick

Every idea I had looked right until I checked it. Each one failed or narrowed when it met the evidence. A pilot is how I'd stop guessing with Uber's money.

Prototypes

I built one prototype for each arm.

Explanation arm: the driver assistant

Answers "the rider paid $X, I got $Y, why?" on 40 synthetic trips. The AI writes the words; the app fills every amount from the trip and rejects any number the AI typed itself.

95% of trips pass every check, up from 60% on my first prompt. No unchecked number reached a driver.

Try the assistant
Promise arm: the cost simulator

Pick a monthly cap and an assumption about how drivers work, and see how often the promise pays out and what it costs, on 15.1 million real Uber trips in NYC.

At 35%: 0% to 4.6% of driver-months topped up, costing at most about 0.7% of Uber's NYC fees.

Decisions

What I chose, and what I threw out.

Limits

What this doesn't prove.

All trip-level numbers come from one city with a driver pay floor, so Uber's share there runs lower than elsewhere, and the cap would cost more in other cities. Public data has no driver IDs, so the promise cost is a range from three assumptions, not a measurement. The assistant runs on synthetic trips, and its 40-case eval set is a starting point, not a launch gate. National figures come from Gridwise's driver network, which may not represent every driver. My two drivers are two people: they're why I looked, not the evidence. With Uber's data, the conclusions could change, which is the point of the pilot.
